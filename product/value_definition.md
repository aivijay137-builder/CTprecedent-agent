# Part 4: Value definition for the top five junctures

Junctures from the Part 2 ranking: 7 (monitoring rivals), 2 (landscaping), 9 (readout and date slips), 4 (feasibility and sites), 3 (protocol design). Everything about what buyers say or do is [HYPOTHESIS] to be tested in interviews unless it cites a source. Data numbers come from Part 3.

How to read "job to be done": **functional** is the task, **emotional** is how the person wants to feel, **social** is how they want to look to others.

## Juncture 7: Mid-study monitoring of rivals

| Item | Definition |
|---|---|
| Buyer | CI analyst (pharma), or the combined CEO / head of clinical ops / BD in a small biotech (Part 1: no dedicated CI role in small biotech postings) |
| Job: functional | Know within a day when a rival's trial stops, slips or shrinks, and know whether it matters to me |
| Job: emotional | Not be surprised in a board or partner meeting |
| Job: social | Be the person who already knew, with a one-line answer ready |
| Minimum output worth paying for | Weekly or same-day message per watchlist item: what changed (with the date the event really happened), the stated reason quoted from the record, a tier label (stated by sponsor / supported by outside source / unknown), the reader-specific "so what" in one sentence, one next step. Example of the bar: items 2 and 10 in Part 3, section 3.4 |
| What "wrong" looks like | Calling a rival's stop a "safety signal" when the sponsor stated a business reason (Part 3 item 8 and 3: stated reasons may be defensive but must be quoted, not overridden). Or reporting an old event as new (items 4 and 9 were updated years late) |
| Cost of wrong | A wrong "rival is out" read could delay a financing or partnership decision or cause a wrong competitor assumption in a board deck. No sourced dollar figure [ASSUMPTION: cost is reputational and one-off, hard to price] |
| Trust signals | Quote the record verbatim, link to the source page, show the date of the event vs the date of the record update, label inference vs fact, show "not found" openly. Human review: not needed per alert in v1, needed for any alert flagged "possible quiet exit" |
| Success metric | User forwards the alert to a colleague or pastes it into a deck. User says "I already knew, but this saved me the lookup" or "I didn't know". Measure: weekly open rate above 60% and at least 1 forward per user per month [ASSUMPTION targets] |

## Juncture 2: Competitive landscaping

| Item | Definition |
|---|---|
| Buyer | BD or portfolio strategy lead; strategy consultant; small-biotech CEO before a financing |
| Job: functional | Produce a landscape slide or memo for one indication that is current and defensible |
| Job: emotional | Confidence that nothing important was missed |
| Job: social | Look prepared in front of the board, investors or a client |
| Minimum output worth paying for | One page for one indication: trials by phase and status, new entrants since the last brief, stops with reasons grouped by cause (using Part 3 tiers), three sentences on what it means, and a CSV behind it. Delivered weekly or on request in under 10 minutes |
| What "wrong" looks like | Missing a competitor because drug names are free text; counting lung-cancer trials as respiratory (23.5% of the respiratory tag, Part 3); stale "unknown" status trials counted as live (10,694 in the bundle) |
| Cost of wrong | A landscape slide with a missing rival in front of an investor. Not priced [ASSUMPTION: reputational] |
| Trust signals | Query definition shown (which conditions, which statuses), counts that reconcile to the raw list, an exclusion list ("we left out cancer trials and 10,694 unverified"), date of data pull |
| Success metric | Slide goes into the deck unchanged apart from branding. Time to produce falls from the vendor-claimed 30 to 60 minutes per competitor per week (vendor claim, Part 2) to under 10 minutes total [ASSUMPTION] |

## Juncture 9: Readout and date slips (investor use)

| Item | Definition |
|---|---|
| Buyer | Biotech fund analyst, sell-side associate, retail or small-fund investor |
| Job: functional | Know whether a catalyst date is still real and whether a slip is a warning sign |
| Job: emotional | Avoid being the last to learn about a delay |
| Job: social | Look sharper than peers on a catalyst call |
| Minimum output worth paying for | For each held name: primary completion date now vs earlier, how many times it moved (needs snapshots), the sponsor's own slip history, and a plain statement of what typically follows. The last part must be computed from data before it is claimed: the share of slips that ended in termination can only be measured once snapshots exist. Until then, say "not yet measured" |
| What "wrong" looks like | A slip labelled "bad sign" with no base rate behind it; registry dates that lag company statements (Part 2, stage 9) |
| Cost of wrong | A mispriced position. Size depends on the position. Not computable without a case [ASSUMPTION needed from an interview] |
| Trust signals | Show the registry date next to the latest company disclosure date, base rates with sample size, no price targets or advice |
| Success metric | User cites the tool in an investment note, or gets the alert before the news wire. Measure: share of alerts that preceded the first press mention (check manually on 20 cases) |

## Juncture 4: Feasibility and site selection

| Item | Definition |
|---|---|
| Buyer | CRO feasibility lead / feasibility manager; site feasibility lead; research coordinator at a site deciding whether to accept a trial |
| Job: functional | For a new trial, say which sites or countries are likely to enroll and who else is fishing in the same pond |
| Job: emotional | Not be blamed for sites that enroll nobody |
| Job: social | Defend a site list in a bid defense (the meeting where a CRO presents its bid) |
| Minimum output worth paying for | For a condition and region: list of other recruiting trials by site and city, past trials at each candidate site with their end status (completed, terminated, why), and a short risk flag per site. Table plus 3 sentences. Delivered within hours, since CRO proposals assemble feasibility inside about 10 working days (PharmaLive, Part 2) |
| What "wrong" looks like | Site names spelled differently across records (the same hospital counted as several), trials counted as competing when they enroll a different population. A wrong "this site is crowded" reading drops a good site |
| Cost of wrong | Tufts 2013 data: about 11% of sites enrolled none and 37% under-enrolled (Part 2). Cost per dead site: not found. Interview a feasibility lead for it |
| Trust signals | Site name matching shown with confidence, eligibility overlap check, list of the competing trials linked to the records |
| Success metric | Feasibility lead pastes the table into a proposal. Measure: used in at least one live proposal within 30 days |

## Juncture 3: Protocol design

| Item | Definition |
|---|---|
| Buyer | Head of clinical operations or clinical development lead in a small biotech or mid-size sponsor; medical writer / clinical scientist drafting the protocol |
| Job: functional | Avoid a design that comparable trials already showed stalls, and justify choices to a regulator or board |
| Job: emotional | Reduce fear of an expensive amendment later |
| Job: social | Show the study team and the board that the design is grounded in precedent |
| Minimum output worth paying for | For a draft protocol (condition, phase, population, key criteria, endpoints, target size): a table of 10 to 30 comparable trials with their outcome (completed, terminated, why), the share stopped for enrollment reasons among comparables (Part 3 gives 33.7% across all stopped bundle trials as the baseline), three flagged risks with the matching records, and a plain list of endpoints used. Must say which claims are record-stated and which are inferred |
| What "wrong" looks like | Comparables chosen wrongly (different population), strictness scored by a crude text match, and a confident "your criteria are too strict" with no precedent shown. A wrong warning can lead the team to loosen criteria and weaken the trial, or to ignore a real risk |
| Cost of wrong | Sourced: median direct cost of a substantial amendment $141,000 (phase II) and $535,000 (phase III); 57% of protocols had at least one; 45% of those were judged avoidable (Tufts 2016, Part 2). Share a tool could prevent: [ASSUMPTION: below 10%] |
| Trust signals | Every flag links to a record; confidence per flag; "comparables used" list the user can edit; human review step by a medical reviewer for the first customers (done by the builder, as a paid service) |
| Success metric | User says "I changed criterion X because of trial Y" or "we added a site list in country Z". Measure: one documented protocol change per customer in the first 60 days |

## Cross-cutting rules for all five

1. **Always show what the record says before what the tool thinks.** Quoted text first, inference second, labelled.
2. **Three confidence labels only:** stated in the record, supported by an outside source (linked), unknown.
3. **Say "not found" out loud.** Part 3 shows 3 of 10 examples could not be explained. A tool that says so earns trust.
4. **Show the real event date next to the record update date.** Part 3 items 4 and 9 show records updated years late.
5. **Exclude cancer by default for respiratory and infectious bundles and say so.**
